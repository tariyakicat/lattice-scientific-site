## What are Elsevier's graphical abstract requirements?

Elsevier asks authors to submit an original image that summarises the paper clearly, reads in an obvious direction, and remains legible when reduced for online display. The publisher's [current graphical abstract guidance](https://www.elsevier.com/en-gb/researcher/author/tools-and-resources/graphical-abstract) gives a useful baseline, but the target journal's Guide for Authors is the final authority.

The standard guidance currently covers five practical areas:

1. submit the graphical abstract as a separate file in the online submission system;
2. use an image of at least 1328 × 531 pixels at 300 dpi, or a larger file with the same ratio;
3. create a clear start and end, usually reading from left to right or top to bottom;
4. remove clutter, unnecessary white space, and a redundant "Graphical abstract" heading;
5. use an accepted file type and obtain permission for third-party material.

These are production requirements, not a ready-made visual formula. A technically compliant image can still fail if the message is crowded, the reading path is ambiguous, or the labels disappear at table-of-contents size.

<Callout title="Check the journal, not only the publisher">
  Open the exact journal's Guide for Authors immediately before export. Submission fields, accepted formats, generative-AI policies, and artwork rules can change independently of Elsevier's general guidance.
</Callout>

## What size should an Elsevier graphical abstract be?

The standard minimum is 1328 × 531 pixels at 300 dpi, using an approximate 500:200 aspect ratio. Elsevier explains that the artwork will be scaled proportionally to fit a 500 × 200-pixel viewing window on ScienceDirect.

That reduction is the part authors often underestimate. A wide canvas may look spacious at full resolution while still containing labels that become unreadable at 200 pixels high. Design at full size, then test a reduced preview before submission.

Do not solve legibility by filling every empty area. White space separates stages, evidence, and outcomes. The goal is to remove unused margins while preserving enough breathing room for a reader to follow the story.

## Which file types and fonts should you use?

Elsevier lists TIFF, EPS, PDF, and Microsoft Office files among its preferred formats and recommends Times, Arial, Courier, or Symbol at a size that remains readable after reduction. The most useful choice depends on how the artwork was built.

- **TIFF** is suitable for a flattened raster export when all text and linework remain sharp at the required resolution.
- **EPS or PDF** can preserve vector shapes and typography, subject to the journal's upload rules.
- **Microsoft Office files** may be practical for artwork created in PowerPoint, but they should be checked for font substitution and shifted objects.

Embed or outline fonts when the workflow allows it, then inspect the exported file on another device. A PDF that looks correct on the design computer can still contain missing characters, altered symbols, or substituted fonts.

Chemical notation, mathematical symbols, gene names, and superscripts deserve a separate proofreading pass. They may survive visual review at full size but become confusing after scaling.

## What should the graphical abstract contain?

An Elsevier graphical abstract should communicate the paper's context, method or mechanism, and main outcome without becoming a miniature manuscript. The publisher describes graphical abstracts as concise pictorial summaries intended to help an interdisciplinary audience understand the take-home message.

A reliable content structure is:

- **Context:** the system, disease, material, or question;
- **Action:** the experiment, intervention, process, or comparison;
- **Outcome:** the principal result or proposed relationship;
- **Direction:** one obvious route through those elements.

Supporting controls, secondary findings, extensive legends, and procedural detail usually belong in the paper. Every element in the graphical abstract should answer one of two questions: "What is happening?" or "Why does it matter?"

If the artwork has several equally strong starting points, the reader will hesitate. Use position, scale, alignment, and contrast to make the first step unmistakable before adding arrows.

## What should you leave out?

Leave out decorative headings, repeated explanations, dense legends, and details that do not change the main conclusion. Elsevier specifically advises against unnecessary white space and the heading "Graphical abstract" inside the image.

Also review:

- unlicensed icons, photographs, or third-party diagrams;
- generative elements that conflict with the publisher or journal's AI policy;
- colour-only distinctions that are inaccessible to some readers;
- low-resolution screenshots copied from the manuscript;
- tiny axis labels or molecular annotations;
- claims that are stronger than the results support.

The [W3C contrast guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum) is useful when checking text over coloured shapes or imagery. Although journal artwork is not a conventional webpage, the same principle applies: essential labels must remain distinguishable from their background.

## How do you prepare the file for submission?

Prepare the file by separating scientific review from technical export review. Combining both in one final glance makes errors easier to miss.

### Scientific review

- confirm that the main claim matches the manuscript;
- verify structures, pathways, labels, quantities, and directionality;
- remove unsupported causal language;
- check terminology and abbreviations against the paper.

### Visual review

- identify the first, second, and final reading points;
- reduce the image to its expected display size;
- test colour contrast and grayscale differentiation;
- check alignment, spacing, and arrow meaning.

### Export review

- confirm dimensions, resolution, and file type;
- inspect the exported file rather than only the source document;
- verify embedded fonts and transparent backgrounds;
- use the journal's required filename and upload field.

<Callout title="A simple final test">
  Show the reduced image to a colleague for ten seconds. If they cannot identify the research subject and principal outcome, revise the hierarchy before submission.
</Callout>

## Is a visual abstract the same thing?

Publishers often use "visual abstract" and "graphical abstract" interchangeably, but some organisations use visual abstract for a more editorial, text-led summary. The label matters less than the required format, audience, and submission instructions.

Our detailed comparison of [visual abstracts and graphical abstracts](/blog/visual-abstract-vs-graphical-abstract/) explains the practical differences in structure and use. When a journal uses a specific term, follow that journal's examples and specifications rather than relying on a universal definition.

## What is the final Elsevier submission checklist?

The final checklist is short enough to use during every export:

- [ ] The target journal currently accepts or requests a graphical abstract.
- [ ] The image communicates one defensible take-home message.
- [ ] The reading direction is obvious.
- [ ] The canvas is at least 1328 × 531 pixels at 300 dpi.
- [ ] Labels remain legible at approximately 500 × 200 pixels.
- [ ] The chosen file type is accepted by the journal.
- [ ] Third-party material and AI-assisted elements comply with policy.
- [ ] There is no redundant "Graphical abstract" heading.
- [ ] The exported file has been opened and checked independently.
- [ ] A scientific reviewer has approved the final content.
